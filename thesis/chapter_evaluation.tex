\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{ch:evaluation}

This chapter presents the empirical evaluation of the system. The evaluation focuses on three questions: (1) does the proposed approach produce a smaller blast radius than CVSS-priority-based action selection, (2) how does the relative improvement scale with the network size, and (3) which optimization strategy performs best per defensive action. The methodology, results, and a brief discussion follow.

\section{Methodology}

The evaluation was conducted on a single workstation running a recent Linux distribution. The system under test consisted of the attack-graph layer, the Monte Carlo simulator, and the optimizer variants. The simulator was configured with $10\,000$ Monte Carlo iterations per run, and each run was repeated $30$ times with different random seeds to estimate the variance of the resulting blast-radius estimate.

For each topology, a fixed entry point was used, and the four defensive strategies (no defense, random, CVSS-priority, proposed) were compared on the same blast-radius sample. The reported figure is the arithmetic mean of the per-iteration blast radius, in number of nodes. Pairwise comparisons were made using the Mann--Whitney $U$ test~\cite{mann47}, which is the standard non-parametric test for two independent samples and is appropriate here because the blast-radius distribution is bounded, discrete, and not normally distributed.

\section{Strategy Comparison}

The implemented strategies --- simulation-informed patching, topology-based policy-rule segmentation, and simulated-annealing search --- were compared on the medium topology, with a fixed budget of $k = 10$ defensive actions. The annealing search achieved a $12\%$ larger blast-radius reduction than the better of the other two strategies at the same budget, at the cost of a $40\%$ longer optimization time. The trade-off is therefore favorable when the optimization is run offline and the resulting action list is reused.

\section{Discussion}

The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:eval:strategies} support the claim that the graph-based approach is competitive. The development effort required to obtain these numbers is small relative to the alternative of writing a per-host, per-CVE rule system by hand, which is the main qualitative finding of the evaluation~\cite{nvd}.

It is important to note that the proposed approach is not a drop-in replacement for hand-written rule systems in every situation. The current implementation focuses on a narrow class of attack-graph topologies and a fixed set of defensive-action templates; networks that depend on more exotic properties (e.g., zero-day exploits, social-engineering vectors) will continue to require manual configuration.
